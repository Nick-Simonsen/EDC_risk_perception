\subsection{S1. Questionnaire Items}
Participants rated the following 18 items in randomized order for nine attributes shown in sequential order (voluntariness of risk, immediacy of effect, knowledge about risk [persons exposed to EDCs], knowledge about risk [known to science], control over risk, newness, chronic-catastrophic, common-dread, and severity of consequences). Below is the exact wording of the task, adapted from \citet{fischhoff1978safe}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Do people get into these risky situations voluntarily? (1 = voluntary; 7 = involuntary)
    \item To what extent is the risk of death immediate-or is death likely to occur at some later time? (1 = immediate; 7 = delayed)
    \item To what extent are the risks known precisely by the persons who are exposed to those risks? (1 = known precisely; 7 = not known)
    \item To what extent are the risks known to science? (1 = known precisely; 7 = not known)
    \item If you are exposed to the risk of each activity to what extent can you, by personal skill or diligence, avoid death while engaging in the activity? (1 = uncontrollable; 7 = controllable)
    \item Are these risks new, novel ones or old, familiar ones? (1 = new; 7 = old)
    \item Is this a risk that kills people one at a time (chronic risk) or a risk that kills large numbers of people at once (catastrophic risk)? (1 = chronic; 7 = catastrophic)
    \item Is this a risk that people have learned to live with and can think about reasonably calmly, or is it one that people have great dread for -- on the level of a gut reaction? (1 = common; 7 = dread)
    \item When the risk from the activity is realized in the form of a mishap or illness, how likely is it that the consequence will be fatal? (1 = certain not to be fatal; 7 = certain to be fatal)
\end{enumerate}\leavevmode

Participants rated the following eighteen exposure scenarios across the nine attributes described above.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Drinking water from plastic bottles that contain Bisphenol A (BPA)
    \item Eating food with phthalates transferred from plastic packaging
    \item Taking pharmaceutical products that contain parabens used as preservatives
    \item Eating foods that contain per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS)
    \item Consuming fish caught in waters contaminated with Brominated Flame Retardants (BFRs)
    \item Eating fruits and vegetables treated with pesticides
    \item Applying body lotions containing Bisphenol A (BPA) directly to the skin
    \item Using deodorants with phthalates in their fragrance
    \item Using sunscreens that contain parabens as preservatives
    \item Applying face creams containing per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) directly to the skin
    \item Sleeping on a mattress that contains Brominated Flame Retardants (BFRs)
    \item Using insect repellents with pesticides on the skin
    \item Breathing in Bisphenol A (BPA) particles from indoor dust
    \item Breathing air containing phthalates from indoor air fresheners
    \item Breathing in paraben particles from indoor dust
    \item Breathing in per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) particles while spending time in a carpeted indoor space
    \item Breathing in brominated flame retardants (BFRs) released from electronic devices
    \item Breathing air containing pesticides that have migrated from landfills
\end{enumerate}\leavevmode

Individual item-ratings for each country are shown in Tables \ref{table:likert-descriptives_UK}-\ref{table:likert-descriptives_US} below.

\subsection{S2. Questionnaire Items for Perceived Susceptibility, Severity, and Overall Health Risk}
To assess participants' perceived health risk from EDC exposure, participants were presented with the following prompt and asked to respond to three items using 7-point Likert scales (1 = very unlikely; 7 = very likely):

\emph{Imagine you have taken no action to reduce your exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals.}

1. How likely are you to become ill from endocrine-disrupting chemicals in the next 12 months
\\2. If you were to become ill from endocrine-disrupting chemicals, how serious do you think this illness would be?
\\3. In general, how big a health risk do you think endocrine-disrupting chemicals pose to your health?

\subsection{S4. Repeated-Measures Robustness Check Using Friedman's Test}
Because rankings were nested within participants, the independence assumption required by the Kruskal-Wallis analysis was not strictly met. As a robustness check, we therefore re-analyzed the data using a within-subjects rank-based omnibus test (Friedman's test; blocking on participant), followed by paired Wilcoxon signed-rank \textit{post hoc} comparisons with Bonferroni corrections. Omnibus effect sizes are reported as Kendall's $W$, and pairwise effects as the rank-biserial correlation $r_rb$, where the sign indicates the direction for the contrast as defined (group 1 vs. group 2).

The repeated-measures results were consistent across countries. The omnibus Friedman test was significant for all three countries (Denmark: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_DK/Friedman_full_result}; UK: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_UK/Friedman_full_result}; US: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_US/Friedman_full_result}). For all three countries, the pairwise contrasts were also significant after Bonferroni corrections for application vs. inhalation (Denmark: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_DK/application_vs_inhalation};
UK: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_UK/application_vs_inhalation};
US: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_US/application_vs_inhalation}), consumption vs. inhalation (Denmark: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_DK/consumption_vs_inhalation}; 
UK: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_UK/consumption_vs_inhalation};
US: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_US/consumption_vs_inhalation}), and consumption vs. application 
(Denmark: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_DK/consumption_vs_application};
UK: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_UK/consumption_vs_application};
US: \input{Results/Friedman_Results_US/consumption_vs_application}).

Overall, this robustness check accounting for within-participant dependence reproduces the inferences drawn from the primary approach. Activity type reliably differentiates responses in all three national samples, with the same ordering: consumption being perceived as the riskiest, inhalation intermediately, and dermal application perceived as the least risky (we remind the reader here that greater means indicate less perceived risk due to reverse coding), with statistically reliable pairwise separations throughout.

\subsection{S5. R-index Analysis of EDC Exposure Scenarios}

To further examine the consistency of perceived importance across individual EDC exposure scenarios, we conducted an exploratory R-index analysis. This analysis provides a measure of the relative importance of each exposure scenario based on pairwise dominance probabilities and allows us to assess whether cross-national differences reflect shifts within exposure categories (consumption, inhalation, application) or broader differences between categories.

We adapted the R-index proposed by \citet{o1992understanding} and previously applied by \citet{lagerkvist2013consumer}. The R-index expresses the probability that one exposure scenario $i$ is more likely to be ranked as riskier than another exposure scenario $j$ based on their observed rank orderings across participants. For each pair of scenarios, the dominance probability is computed as:

\begin{equation}
R_{i,j} = \frac{A + B}{A + 2B + C} \times 100,
\end{equation}

where $A$ is the total number of times scenario $i$ is ranked higher than scenario $j$, $B$ is the number of ties between $i$ and $j$, and $C$ is the total number of times scenario $j$ is ranked higher than scenario $i$. These quantities are calculated as:

\begin{align}
A &= a_{1}(b_{2} + \cdots + z_{2}) + b_{1}(c_{2} + \cdots + z_{2}) + \cdots + w_{1} z_{2}, \\
B &= \tfrac{1}{2}(a_{1}a_{2} + \cdots + z_{1}z_{2}), \\
C &= a_{2}(b_{1} + \cdots + z_{1}) + b_{2}(c_{1} + \cdots + z_{1}) + \cdots + w_{2} z_{1},
\end{align}

where the subscripts denote ranking frequencies for each scenario.

Pairwise dominance probabilities were computed for all $n$ exposure scenarios, producing an R-index matrix that reflects the stochastic dominance relations among exposure scenarios. A scenario with a high R-index score is more likely to be ranked as riskier over other scenarios and can therefore be interpreted as having greater perceived importance. Summing across each row of the matrix yields an overall dominance score for each exposure scenario, which can be used to derive a stochastic rank order.

The resulting R-index rankings were highly consistent across the US, Denmark, and the UK (Table \ref{table:rindex_combined}). Cross-national differences primarily reflected reordering within exposure categories rather than disagreement about which exposure categories were most concerning. For example, participants in the United States tended to rate pesticide-treated produce and BPA in drinking water as more concerning than participants in Denmark, while in all three countries dermal application, such as paraben-based sunscreens, and BFR-treated mattresses were consistently placed among the least risky exposure scenarios.

\subsection{S6. Regression Diagnostics and Robustness Checks}

When model diagnostics indicated nonlinearity, outcomes were log-transformed prior to estimation. This applied only to perceived severity in the US sample. When the Breusch-Pagan test indicated heteroscedasticity, models were estimated with heteroscedasticity-consistent robust standard errors using HC covariance estimation \citep{hayes2007using,long2000using,wilcox2011introduction}.

Shapiro-Wilk tests indicated deviations from normality for perceived severity in the Danish (\input{Results/Regression_Results_Danish/Severity_shapiro_test}) and UK samples (\input{Results/Regression_Results_UK/Severity_shapiro_test}), and for all three outcomes in the US sample (overall health risk: \input{Results/Regression_Results_US/Exposure_shapiro_test}; severity: \input{Results/Regression_Results_US/Severity_shapiro_test}; susceptibility: \input{Results/Regression_Results_US/Susceptibility_shapiro_test}). 

Because violations of residual normality are of limited concern in large-sample linear regression \citep{knief2021violating,gelman2007data}, these deviations were not considered problematic. Visual inspection of Q-Q plots (Plots \ref{fig:qq_us}-\ref{fig:qq_uk}) indicated approximate normality with only minor deviations in the distribution tails, a pattern that is known to inflate Shapiro-Wilk sensitivity.

All regression coefficients are reported in Tables \ref{table:regression_results_Danish}-\ref{table:regression_results_UK}.

\subsection{S7. Intra- and Inter-Country Differences in Perceived Overall Health Risk, Severity, and Susceptibility}

As an exploratory analysis, we examined whether perceived overall health risk, perceived severity, and perceived susceptibility to EDCs differed within and between countries. To this end, we fitted a mixed-design ANOVA with \textit{Category} (perceived overall health risk, perceived severity, perceived susceptibility) as a within-subject factor and \textit{Country} (Denmark, UK, US) as a between-subject factor. Descriptive statistics for each category by country are shown in Table \ref{table:health_descriptives}.

\input{Results/health_descriptives}

Because Levene's test indicated heterogeneity of variance, we supplemented the conventional mixed-design ANOVA with heteroscedasticity-robust methods. Following recommendations by \cite{delacre2019taking}, we implemented a Welch-adjusted approximate degrees of freedom (Welch-ADF) omnibus test using 20\% trimmed means and Winsorized variances, which provides improved control of Type I error rates under variance heterogeneity.

The mixed ANOVA showed a statistically significant main effect of Category (Welch on subject means: \input{Results/Mixed_Anova_Results/category_main_effect}), a statistically significant main effect of Country (\input{Results/Mixed_Anova_Results/country_main_effect}), and a statistically significant Category $\times$ Country interaction (\input{Results/Mixed_Anova_Results/interaction_main_effect}). Welch-ADF omnibus tests corroborated these findings (see Table \ref{table:wj_table}).

To further evaluate cross-country differences in category effects while reducing sensitivity to outliers and variance heterogeneity, we used Yuen's trimmed-mean test on subject-level difference scores \citep{yuen1974two}. This procedure replaces sample means with 20\% trimmed means and uses Winsorized variances, yielding robust inference under skewed distributions and unequal variances. Simulation studies show that this approach maintains accurate Type I error rates while retaining good statistical power \citep{wilcox2017modern}.

Results of Yuen's trimmed-mean interaction contrasts are reported in Table \ref{table:ipw_table}. All post hoc contrasts were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm correction \citep{holm1979simple}, which controls the familywise error rate more efficiently than the Bonferroni procedure.

\subsection{S8. Consistency and Exposure-Pathway Modeling}

Two exploratory analyses were conducted to examine (i) the consistency between perceived likelihood of becoming ill (\emph{susceptibility}) and perceived seriousness of illness (\emph{severity}), and (ii) the relationship between perceived overall health risk (\emph{overall risk}) and pathway-specific rankings of perceived health risk (consumption, inhalation, and dermal application).

\paragraph{Consistency Between Susceptibility and Severity.}
To assess the association between susceptibility and severity, we computed polychoric correlations with SE-based 95\% confidence intervals as well as Spearman's $\rho$ with 95\% confidence intervals. Agreement was further evaluated using paired differences (Susceptibility $-$ Severity) with two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Cohen's $d_z$, the effect-size $r$, and the proportion of participants with absolute differences of at most one scale point.

Across all three countries, susceptibility and severity were moderately and positively associated based on both polychoric and Spearman correlations (Denmark: \input{Results/Consistency_Health_Results_DK/consistency_health_DK_association}; UK: \input{Results/Consistency_Health_Results_UK/consistency_health_UK_association}; US: \input{Results/Consistency_Health_Results_US/consistency_health_US_association}). Agreement analyses further revealed a consistent positive offset (Susceptibility $-$ Severity $> 0$), indicating that participants tended to rate their likelihood of becoming ill as higher than the perceived seriousness of the illness itself (Denmark: \input{Results/Consistency_Health_Results_DK/consistency_health_DK_agreement}; UK: \input{Results/Consistency_Health_Results_UK/consistency_health_UK_agreement}; US: \input{Results/Consistency_Health_Results_US/consistency_health_US_agreement}).

\paragraph{Linking Pathway-Specific Ranking of Perceived Health Risk to Perceived Overall Health Risk.}
To relate perceived overall health risk to pathway-specific perceived health risk rankings, scores for consumption ($S_{\mathrm{cons}}$), application ($S_{\mathrm{app}}$), and inhalation ($S_{\mathrm{inh}}$) were derived from the global rankings of the 18 exposure scenarios. Global ranks $r \in \{1,\dots,18\}$ were converted to Borda scores ($b = 19 - r$) and averaged within pathways.

Bivariate Spearman correlations between perceived overall health risk and each pathway score were uniformly small across countries (Denmark: \input{Results/Exposure_CLM_Results_DK/exposure_clm_DK_spearman}; UK: \input{Results/Exposure_CLM_Results_UK/exposure_clm_UK_spearman}; US: \input{Results/Exposure_CLM_Results_US/exposure_clm_US_spearman}); $p$-values were Holm-adjusted within each country across the three pathway-specific correlations (consumption, application, inhalation).

To further test whether pathway contrasts predicted perceived overall health risk, we fitted cumulative link models (CLMs; logit link) using two standardized, collinearity-free contrasts:
\[
C_{\mathrm{cons\text{--}inh}} = \mathrm{scale}(S_{\mathrm{cons}} - S_{\mathrm{inh}}), \quad
C_{\mathrm{app\text{--}inh}} = \mathrm{scale}(S_{\mathrm{app}} - S_{\mathrm{inh}}).
\]
We report per-standard-deviation odds ratios (ORs) with Wald 95\% confidence intervals and $z$ and $p$-values. The proportional-odds assumption was evaluated using likelihood-ratio tests against partial proportional-odds models with cutpoint-specific effects. Model fit was summarized using likelihood-ratio tests against the null model and McFadden's $R^2$.

The CLMs showed little evidence of predictive value in Denmark (\input{Results/Exposure_CLM_Results_DK/exposure_clm_DK_omnibus}) and the UK (\input{Results/Exposure_CLM_Results_UK/exposure_clm_UK_omnibus}), with only marginal omnibus fit in the US (\input{Results/Exposure_CLM_Results_US/exposure_clm_US_omnibus}). Per-standard-deviation ORs were near unity in Denmark (\input{Results/Exposure_CLM_Results_DK/exposure_clm_DK_location_effects}) and the UK (\input{Results/Exposure_CLM_Results_UK/exposure_clm_UK_location_effects}). In the US, a small inverse association was observed for the Application vs.\ Inhalation contrast (OR $< 1$; \input{Results/Exposure_CLM_Results_US/exposure_clm_US_location_effects}).

For the UK and US, proportional-odds assumptions were satisfied, and reported ORs represent uniform effects across cutpoints. In Denmark, the proportional-odds assumption was violated for the Consumption vs. Inhalation contrast; the reported OR therefore reflects an average across thresholds. Because the corresponding confidence interval included 1, substantive conclusions were unchanged. The Application vs. Inhalation contrast satisfied the proportional-odds assumption and showed no significant effect.

Taken together, susceptibility and severity were consistently correlated but systematically offset, with participants judging their susceptibility to be higher than the severity of illness. In contrast, aggregated pathway-specific perceived health risk rankings showed little association with perceived overall health risk in Denmark and the UK, and only a small, inverse association for Application vs. Inhalation in the US. These findings indicate that pathway-level perceived-health-risk rankings and perceived overall health risk are largely distinct, with at most a weak country-specific signal in the US.

\subsection{S9. Exploratory Test of Chemical-Label and Exposure-Pathway Effects}
As an exploratory analysis on whether pathway-specific patterns were consistent with a label-fluency interpretation, we re-analyzed perceived risk scores using individual chemical labels. Participants' rankings were transformed into Borda scores to permit comparison of relative perceived risk across chemical-label-by-pathway combinations, where higher Borda scores indicated higher perceived risk. For each country, we fitted a linear model where Borda scores were modeled as a function of chemical label, exposure pathway (consumption, inhalation, and dermal application), and their interaction. Chemical labels included pesticides, BPA, phthalates, parabens, PFAS, and BFRs. The omnibus model, testing whether perceived health risk varied across the 18 chemical-label-by-pathway combinations, was statistically significant in all three countries (Denmark \input{Results/Chemical_Label_Effect_Results/chemical_label_omnibus_DK}; UK \input{Results/Chemical_Label_Effect_Results/chemical_label_omnibus_UK}; US \input{Results/Chemical_Label_Effect_Results/chemical_label_omnibus_US}), indicating that perceived health risk varied systematically across chemical labels and exposure pathways.

Post-hoc Holm-adjusted estimated marginal mean contrasts were then used to compare each technical chemical label against pesticides within each exposure pathway, with standard errors clustered by participant (see Tables \ref{table:chemical_label_contrasts_DK}-\ref{table:chemical_label_contrasts_US}). These contrasts did not support the interpretation that technical chemical labels were uniformly perceived as riskier than \emph{pesticides}. For consumption-related scenarios, some technical labels were rated as higher than or did not differ significantly from pesticides. PFAS and BFRs were rated higher than pesticides in Denmark, PFAS was rated higher than pesticides in the UK, and PFAS and BFRs did not differ significantly from pesticides in the US. By contrast, for dermal application and inhalation scenarios, pesticides were rated as significantly riskier than each individual technical chemical label in all three countries. Thus, although processing fluency may still have contributed to judgments of specific labels, the observed pattern was more consistent with systematic differences across exposure pathways than with a general disfluency-based increase in perceived health risk.
