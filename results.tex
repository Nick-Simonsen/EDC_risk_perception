\section{Results}

\subsection{Risk Perception Ratings}
Participants rated 18 EDC-related activities on nine risk attributes commonly used in psychometric risk research (e.g., \citealp{fischhoff1978safe,slovic1987perception}). Descriptive statistics are reported separately for the UK, Denmark, and the US in Tables \ref{table:likert-descriptives_UK}, \ref{table:likert-descriptives_DK}, and \ref{table:likert-descriptives_US}, respectively.

\subsection{Principal Component Analysis of Risk Attributes}
KMO values indicated adequate sampling (US: \input{Results/PCA_Results_US/kmo_test}; Denmark: \input{Results/PCA_Results_DK/kmo_test}; UK: \input{Results/PCA_Results_UK/kmo_test}), and Bartlett's tests confirmed that the correlation matrices differed significantly from the identity matrix (US: \input{Results/PCA_Results_US/bartlett_test}; Denmark: \input{Results/PCA_Results_DK/bartlett_test}; UK: \input{Results/PCA_Results_UK/bartlett_test}). The data were therefore suitable for PCA. Using the Kaiser criterion, two components were retained for each country.

Across all three countries, the same two dimensions of EDC risk perception emerged. The first dimension reflects affectively charged perceptions of risk, characterized by catastrophic potential, dread, fatality, and acute or rapidly lethal outcomes (Affective-Dread dimension). The second dimension reflects more deliberative, knowledge-based perceptions of risk, including voluntariness, familiarity, knowability among the public and scientists, and perceived controllability (Cognitive-Deliberate dimension).

In the UK and the US, the Affective-Dread dimension corresponds to the first extracted component (RC1) and the Cognitive-Deliberate dimension the second (RC2). In Denmark, the same two dimensions are present but component indices are reversed; the Danish RC2 corresponds to the Affective-Dread dimension (i.e., it matches US/UK RC1 in its defining loadings), and the Danish RC1 corresponds to the Cognitive-Deliberate dimension\footnote{Because the sign and order of PCA components are arbitrary, reversing signs or swapping components does not alter the underlying solution \citep{jolliffe2016principal}.}. In the UK and the US, the Affective-Dread dimension accounted for the largest share of variance, whereas in Denmark the Cognitive-Deliberate dimension was more prominent. Thus, cross-national differences reflect variation in the relative salience of shared dimensions rather than differences in the underlying factor solution (see Table \ref{table:pca_loadings_table} and Figure \ref{fig:pca-biplots}).

Across Denmark, the UK, and the US, participants' attribute ratings largely aligned with our \emph{a priori} hypothesis, while also revealing clear boundary conditions. Consistent with our hypothesis, EDC-related activities were generally perceived as delayed rather than immediate risks, supporting the characterization of EDCs as “slow-burn” hazards rather than acute lethal threats. Ratings further indicated a systematic asymmetry in perceived knowledge. Across countries, EDC risks were, as predicted, judged as substantially better understood by scientists than by the general public, consistent with a perceived public-expert knowledge gap. Judgments of chronicity, dread, and fatality clustered near scale midpoints, indicating that EDC risks were not consistently construed as catastrophic, highly dreaded, or certainly fatal. Agency-related attributes showed strong context dependence, with perceived voluntariness and controllability varying markedly across activities, suggesting that EDC risks do not constitute a single coherent profile of involuntary-but-controllable exposure, inconsistent with our hypothesis. Perceived novelty exhibited similar context sensitivity, with pesticide-related exposures distinct from other EDC activities, consistent with our hypothesis that pesticides would be viewed as less novel. Overall, these patterns indicate a broadly shared representation of EDC risks as delayed and unevenly knowable, while differing more substantially across exposure types in perceived agency and novelty (see Table \ref{table:likert-descriptives_combined}). Detailed item-level ratings for each country are reported in Tables \ref{table:likert-descriptives_UK}-\ref{table:likert-descriptives_US} in Supplementary Section S1.

The PCA biplots (Figure \ref{fig:pca-biplots}) suggest a clustering of activities primarily by exposure pathway rather than by specific EDC category. Across all three countries, inhalation related activities group together and are positioned closer to regions associated with low voluntariness, low controllability, and greater perceived uncertainty. This pattern indicates that inhalation exposures are construed less in terms of distinct hazard characteristics and more in terms of ambiguity and limited agency. Consumption related activities form a more spatially dispersed pattern, reflecting greater differentiation across perceived health risks and familiarity. By contrast, application based exposures, such as deodorant, sunscreen, and face creams, cluster tightly together, indicating a shared perceptual profile characterized by relatively lower perceived risk intensity and greater perceived familiarity. The consistency of this exposure-pathway organization across countries highlights exposure pathway as a prominent organizing feature of EDC risk judgment.

\subsection{Perceived Health Risk Across EDC Exposure Pathways}
Across all three countries, perceived EDC-related health risks followed a highly consistent hierarchy across exposure pathways, with consumption-related exposures perceived as most risky, inhalation-related exposures as intermediate, and dermal application exposures as least risky (see Table \ref{table:oneway-anova-pathway-descriptives}).

As a complementary exploratory analysis, we assessed the relative importance of individual exposure scenarios using an adapted R-index based on pairwise dominance probabilities \citep{o1992understanding, lagerkvist2013consumer}. This analysis revealed a highly consistent ordering of exposure scenarios across countries (see Supplementary Table \ref{table:rindex_combined}). Cross-national differences primarily reflected reordering within exposure categories rather than disagreement about which exposure categories were most risky. For example, participants in the US tended to rate pesticide-treated produce and BPA in drinking water somewhat riskier than participants in Denmark, whereas dermal application scenarios were consistently among the least concerning exposures across all three countries. Additional details regarding the \emph{R}-index procedure and item-level dominance matrices are provided in Supplementary Section S5.

An exploratory analysis examined whether pathway-specific patterns could be explained by processing fluency differences across chemical labels. Results suggested that processing fluency alone was unlikely to explain the observed pathway-specific patterns (see Supplementary Section S9).

\subsection{Individual Predictors of Perceived Severity, Susceptibility, and Overall Health Risk to EDCs}
Multiple regression models were used to examine how sociodemographic, cognitive, and psychosocial factors predicted perceived severity, susceptibility, and overall health risk to EDCs across Denmark, the US, and the UK. Models were estimated using transformations and robust standard errors where needed to address assumption violations (see Supplementary Section S6 for full diagnostics).

Across all three countries, self-reported EDC knowledge was the most consistent predictor across the three risk-perception measures. Greater knowledge predicted higher perceived severity across Denmark, the US, and the UK, and was also associated with higher perceived overall health risk in Denmark and the US and higher perceived susceptibility in Denmark. Gender differences emerged primarily in the US and UK, where male participants reported lower perceived severity and overall health risk than female participants. Institutional trust showed country-specific associations, predicting higher perceived severity in the US and UK but lower perceived overall health risk in Denmark.

Other predictors showed less stable patterns across outcomes and countries. Age, income, parenthood, education, perceived health, employment status, familiarity with EDCs, and geographical residence were associated with specific outcomes in some countries, but no consistent cross-national pattern emerged. Therefore, we interpret these findings as localized associations rather than general predictors of EDC risk perceptions. Full model coefficients and fit statistics are reported in Tables \ref{table:regression_results_Danish}-\ref{table:regression_results_UK}.

\subsection{Exploratory Robustness Checks}
As an exploratory analysis, we examined whether perceived overall health risk, severity, and susceptibility differed across countries and across categories. Results indicated significant main effects of category and country, as well as a category-by-country interaction, suggesting that the relative emphasis on overall health risk, severity, and susceptibility varied across countries. Full statistical results are reported in Supplementary Section S7.

As an exploratory robustness check, we examined the consistency between perceived susceptibility and perceived severity and whether pathway-specific rankings of perceived health risks mapped onto perceived overall health risk. Susceptibility and severity were moderately correlated across all three countries, but participants consistently rated susceptibility higher than severity. In contrast, pathway-specific rankings of perceived health risks showed little systematic association with perceived overall health risk. Full statistical results are reported in Supplementary Section S8.
